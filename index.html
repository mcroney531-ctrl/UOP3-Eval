Field Report — One Week In

AI Coaching Product: After One Week

Leadership-facing exploratory evaluation · approximately one week of access

Portfolio adaptation of an original workplace field report. Following an early product rollout, a senior leader invited my assessment; I focused on practical value, user adoption, and what might encourage people to return. This was an early, qualitative hands-on evaluation—not a formal user study. Product details and selected examples have been generalized for confidentiality; observations, design hypotheses, and recommendations are distinguished throughout.

What I Did
  • Attempted direct teammate lookups/calls in the chat session: arguably the most critical feature on the entire app. It was the first thing I asked about when I logged in, and something I kept testing throughout the week. There's a big difference between seeing a continuum, a graph, or a side-by-side comparison of you and a colleague, and having relevant comparison insights available to inform a conversation in more situations — being able to flat-out state, without sugarcoating and without fear of judgment, "I am facing this problem with this colleague," and actually talk or work through it. That's significant, and it was a point of emphasis for me early and often. It was admittedly a bit bumpy when a colleague's profile wasn't available, though the lookup experience improved during my week of access. I treated this as a usability issue worth monitoring.
  • Inquired about the tool's own capabilities and discussed potential use cases with it conversationally: I personally think discussing what a large language model can and can't do, and how it interacts with you, is — or should be — standard practice, and a quick way to align and set expectations. I thought the tool was pretty clear in its communication on this front.
  • Attempted to launch a "project": not always, but I tend to give LLM chat sessions specific purposes — general talk, article drafting, a specific task — and wanted to do the same here: establish a "purpose," a longer-term environment with context set upfront. A common mistake with LLMs is skipping that step entirely, or stopping at the cheesy "you are a world-class expert" framing instead of plainly stating why you're there and what you're doing. Establishing those parameters gives a session far more purpose and precise direction — and a long-term, purposeful setup like this isn't just practical for the user, it inherently encourages them to return. I started a project-oriented conversation with an ongoing work task in mind, with the intention of evaluating how useful that context remained over time.
  • Sought guidance on rewording a (mock) harsh message draft: personally, I think this is one of the cleaner use cases — one that can cut through effectively to a wide range of audiences. We all have things we hold in or don't fully address head-on: maybe there's no proper outlet, maybe we don't want to seem dumb, maybe we're not comfortable naming how a certain behavior is really bothering us, or maybe we just don't feel confident in how we've worded something important. Or the kneejerk instinct is to say it a certain way, when saying it like that would only cause more problems or unintended consequences. This was something I really wanted to try, and I'll keep testing it in real settings — I think there's wide "human" appeal here.

    Doing this also helped me think again in augmentation terms. The results I got back were good, but I rarely take a first draft from AI as final — the way I tend to work with it is to take the output and fine-tune/iterate until I'm happy with the message, or whatever we're putting together. So the natural next thought was: what if I included examples of messages I've actually sent, to infuse my own voice into these draft outputs — along with anything else that could make the output richer or more useful? At that point, you and the coach are operating on a more similar playing field. On a larger scale, you could even turn it on yourself and ask it to "evaluate my messaging style" — and if relevant personal context were available, it could make that exercise more reflective of how you actually communicate. The more you lean into the augmentation, the richer and more valuable the tool starts to feel.
  • Light stress-testing: pushed toward its boundaries — not stress-tested on purpose, just asked in passing — the tool held those too. When I asked about technical details it couldn't access, it set a clear limit. Asked about pasting a colleague's chat exchange in for help, the response was "yes, but" — with a clear caveat to redact sensitive details. Not a blanket refusal, not reckless — good judgment, unprompted, in a moment that wasn't a test.

Early Impressions

  • Top-line, one week in: overall impression is genuinely positive. Real potential is already visible, and use cases are surfacing independently rather than needing to be prompted.
  • This could become an everyday app for many people. Where the existing resource is more of a "touch-consultation" experience, a conversational layer opens up more situations in which someone might find it useful.
  • More versatile than anticipated — with real potential to invite return visits. It can help with things in the moment, big or small, but catered with real specificity. Where the assessment platform informed you of preferences and tendencies broadly — how they might affect a situation in the abstract — the conversational experience could draw on appropriate assessment insights when discussing a specific situation, rather than leaving the user to make the connection alone. That could give someone more reasons to return to the app.
  • These behaviors already exist — and that's a good thing. This ties back to an important concept in my broader AI-adoption thinking, which was that the strongest AI successes and the most transferable use cases are the ones people are already doing, just unstructured — on a personal ChatGPT or Claude account, or their work Copilot account, consulting about something, rewording a message, whatever it is. What's different here isn't the behavior, it's the structure: the possibility of assessment-informed conversations—including appropriate comparison insights—is a meaningful difference from an ad hoc chat without that background. The pitch isn't convincing people to start doing something new — it's pointing to people already doing it and giving that behavior a properly contextualized home.
  • There's real value in comparing and contrasting the assessment platform and the AI coaching app. It goes back to the "what are you already doing — and already doing well" framing that lends itself to successful AI adoption. Thinking about this tool with a tether to familiar terms helped clarify my thinking, but it also made the contrasts click clearly: "oh, X can be an extension of Y" or "wow, this could make the experience behind Z feel so much richer now." Instead of an abstract "AI" or "AI-powered" pitch, it becomes a plain what-is vs. what-can-be.
  • "What if the assessment resource could talk back?" The words that kept coming up all week were static and dynamic — that's the core differentiator, illustrated further in the Scenario below. Framing it as "talking to the assessment platform" makes it feel more familiar to people less comfortable with AI, because the key point isn't anything sci-fi — it's about making previously static insights something a user can discuss and apply. This product, and its dynamic layer, extends what already made the assessment platform great; it doesn't replace it. One possibility I kept coming back to: a familiar reference-first experience and a newer conversational one could eventually serve different preferences, even as they share a foundation.
  • The conversational boundaries held — even off-script. Some of my most useful early impressions came from conversations that weren't planned tests. The app still maintained useful boundaries in those moments.
  • The Augmentation Mindset Shift: while troubleshooting a small issue, it became clear this tool can be used in parallel with work — not just consulted the way a static resource is consulted. Most tools in this category are reference material: here is information, here are tips, here is data, go use it. This one, used well, behaves differently — closer to augmentation than reference, worked with in real time rather than looked up and left. The notable part isn't that the tool can do this — it's that no one was following an anticipated workflow to get there. The conversation surfaced it unprompted.
💡The Key Unlock

This is the floor, not the ceiling.

Out of the box, the app offers a common baseline: an AI coach, standard features, and a reasonable starting experience. What happens after that point is not fixed — the ceiling is set by what the user brings to it and how far they choose to take it. Nearly everything below is either evidence of that floor, or a response to how high the ceiling could go.

Supporting Example — AI Coach Exchange

Me: "I think there's potential — with limits — to use you nicely in the AI-augmented workflow age. Where yes, we discuss how to talk about stuff, but also kinda working 'side-by-side' on things too, if you will. My goal is to think beyond the basics and show my team how, in essence, your program and the features on this app are the floor, but the dynamic nature of AI comms means that it's NOT the ceiling in the same way static content is — in other words, beyond 'what you see is what you get.' I know you can't give internal technical details, but even still, we did some light tech troubleshooting. That might not be a use case, but it illustrates that you can go where the user takes you, within reason."

AI coach response (paraphrased to protect internal product information): The coach reinforced the distinction between the app's visible features and the more flexible support a conversation could provide. It described moving from understanding an interpersonal dynamic to drafting a message, exploring patterns, or planning a practical next step—within the tool's boundaries. It also emphasized transparency: the coach should be clear about what it cannot access, distinguish inference from fact, and encourage verification with the app or a person when appropriate. That combination of practical support and clear limits was the point I wanted to illustrate.

Wishlist

A few rough edges surfaced this week. Framed under the core thesis, these read less as broken promises and more as specific spots where the floor is still settling.

  • Memory across sessions — during my initial testing, stated preferences didn't appear to persist. A limitation in that early experience, not evidence the longer-term potential isn't real.
  • Ability to rename conversations. This goes back to giving a chat a purpose — it's not a one-off conversation, it's the establishment of an environment, and that invites returnability and consistent use.
  • Let the daily tip ask, not assume — in my early tests, it assumed a generic course-participant context rather than adjusting to the actual role. That was a gap in one specific interaction, not evidence the whole product was unreliable. Rather than assuming role and context upfront, a light clarifying question on first use could invite the user to self-customize — self-correcting through interaction instead of requiring a manual rebuild later.

    The daily tip, delivered as a message in a workplace communication tool, feels like an invitation into the full app — which makes it even more critical to get right. If it comes off inaccurate, unhelpful, or stale, it signals that the rest of the experience will be similar. Rather than trying to engineer the "right" context for everyone — which is borderline impossible — making it more conversational and inquiry-based serves as a better invitation, and takes some of that precision pressure off. In the version I tested, responding to the message continued the exchange in the app. That makes the first interaction worth getting right. Asking and arriving at the daily tip/focus together, based on inquiry, makes it feel more collaborative in nature — and encourages the kind of augmented workflow that will make this product a successful one.
  • "Quick actions" — this extends an approach I saw in the app: topic-based buttons that open a coach conversation with a suggested starting prompt. Those are more topic/concept-based; this would be more action-based. Example: Quick actions → "Help me craft a message" → launches a coach conversation with "Great, what is the message, and who are we sending it to?" If research on use cases has already been done, this could be a great way to put that data to use. The tool is extremely vast and can be used in a myriad of ways, which is great but can also be overwhelming. Quick actions could be a fast, practical way for even a light user to say "well, I do use it sometimes, with the help-me-craft-a-message feature" — straightforward, but still carrying all the utility of the tool. It also helps shift the mindset from "this can be informative" to "I can actually do things in here."
  • Explore connecting self-guided learning experiences with AI coaching (proposed). Those modules already invite action — adding conversational support could pair beautifully with that experience. Imagine a learner choosing to bring the goals from an action plan into a coaching conversation, so they could work through how to apply those goals to something happening right now. Instead of treating the module and the conversation as two separate stops, one could give the other more purpose. That's a future design opportunity, not functionality I observed during this first week.

    The same logic could extend to the product's tailored-advice experience. There's something simple and approachable about the basic advice flow: you select a colleague you want advice on working with, plus what specifically you want advice on. That could be made even more interactive, dynamic, and accessible for the average user, precisely because it's already targeted and specific — and familiar to anyone who's used the assessment platform. More of the "what are you already doing well" concept.
04

What This Means for How We Teach It

The traditional model for rolling out a feature is straightforward: the feature is static, a walkthrough explains what it does, and that's sufficient. This product doesn't fit that model cleanly. Its actual ceiling depends entirely on what the user brings to it — a one-time walkthrough won't teach someone how to get long-term value out of something that rewards ongoing use.

The practical implication: learning content likely needs to shift from here's what this feature does to here's how you can make the most of it — teaching usage patterns and habits, not just surface functionality. You only get out what you put in, and that has to be taught deliberately. It won't be assumed correctly on its own.

05

Questions Moving Forward

  • What are the "early wins" a user can experience that build belief in a product and a desire to return to it for genuine guidance?
  • What research was done during development on how people actually use AI at work, and what did it reveal? That's the kind of insight that could surface clear-cut use cases worth showcasing and advertising.
  • Have we considered role-specific use cases? A project manager constantly checking in with reports on their progress or giving them direction is likely to use this quite differently than an individual contributor. This loops back to the research question above, and connects to the "early wins" a user can build on and want to keep returning to.
  • What cultural and social tone should we actively encourage around this product? the assessment platform took real, deliberate effort to build its own culture and language — the shift from "I've done the assessment platform" to "we use the assessment platform." Something I've been personally wondering about AI more broadly: when does it become normal to talk openly about a genuine revelation from a conversation with an AI, without it feeling stigmatized? Will people share their wins with this product openly, or does it feel more private by nature — and what's our role in shaping that language?
  • Like the useful "here's how you can use this to help yourself advance, improve, and progress" framing from a professional-development course — what are the ways we ensure we're presenting this tool in that same light, rather than leaning too heavily on novelty, or a "look what it can do" pitch?
  • What does long-term, consistent QA look like for a product this dynamic and responsive, in an environment where things change quickly and human behavior is unpredictable? Today's novelty can become tomorrow's roadblock, hallucination, or stale feature — how do we ensure consistently high-quality outputs over time?
  • Bonus: not really a question so much as an anticipated one. With our current assessments and products, we already get asked whether they hold up and translate across cultures. AI is known to carry bias — it's essentially impossible to have a fully unbiased LLM, since it's trained on human data and humans hold biases too, so even the cleanest systems still have those loops. I'd anticipate eventually fielding similar questions about cultural crossover, hold-up, and translation with this product's AI layer — good to start thinking about now, ahead of being asked.
